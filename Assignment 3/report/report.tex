\begin{abstract}
We re-implement TIGER~\cite{rajput2023tiger}, a generative-retrieval
sequential recommender that represents each item as a tuple of
discrete \emph{Semantic IDs} and uses an encoder--decoder Transformer
to autoregressively generate the next item's Semantic ID.  Our
pipeline feeds Sentence-T5 content embeddings into a residual-quantised
VAE (RQ-VAE) with $L{=}3$ codebook levels of $K{=}256$ entries, plus
a per-item disambiguation suffix.  A 4+4-layer encoder--decoder
Transformer then beam-decodes the next item.  We evaluate on the
Amazon Toys and Games 5-core dump ($19{,}412$ users, $11{,}924$ items)
under the TIGER full-item-ranking protocol.  We report
Recall@\{5,10\} and NDCG@\{5,10\}, and ablate the RQ-VAE and
Transformer axes plus inference beam size.

The default run (beam~$20$) attains Recall@5 $=$ \textbf{0.0342},
NDCG@5 $=$ \textbf{0.0245}, Recall@10 $=$ \textbf{0.0488}, NDCG@10
$=$ \textbf{0.0292}, just below the assignment's expected band
(R@5 $\approx$ 0.05, NDCG@5 $\approx$ 0.03--0.04).  Our RQ-VAE
\emph{collapsed} on this dataset.  Only $1$, $20$, and $16$ of the
$K{=}256$ codebook entries are used at levels $0$, $1$, $2$.  This
leaves $115$ unique $L$-tuples for $11{,}924$ items, a $99.9\%$
collision rate.  The
disambiguation suffix carries the bulk of the item-identifying signal,
yet only $2.38\%$ of beam outputs are invalid.  Ablation shows the
Semantic-ID-shaping axes ($K$, $L$) dominate. A single $K$ or $L$
mistake costs an order of magnitude in Recall@5, while Transformer
width/depth and beam size move it by only a few absolute percent.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================================
Sequential recommendation predicts the next item a user will interact with
given their chronological history.  Modern systems such as SASRec~\cite{kang2018sasrec}
score items via dense dot-products in an embedding space. At serving time
this is paired with approximate nearest-neighbor search to remain
tractable.  TIGER takes a different route: each item is assigned a tuple
of discrete tokens (its \emph{Semantic ID}), derived from its content
features, and an encoder--decoder Transformer autoregressively
\emph{generates} the Semantic ID of the next item.  No ANN index is
required, semantically similar items naturally share Semantic-ID prefixes,
and items unseen at training time can in principle be recommended through
their Semantic ID alone~\cite{rajput2023tiger}.

\begin{sloppypar}
We re-implement TIGER on Amazon Toys and
Games~\cite{mcauley2015image} and ablate the choices that the original
paper sweeps over.  We also analyse the failure modes of generative
retrieval that embedding-based systems do not have: invalid
generations, codebook collapse, and collision disambiguation.  The
codebook-collapse story turns out to dominate this dataset, and we
report it openly because the disambiguation suffix is doing more
than the paper's framing suggests.
\end{sloppypar}

\section{Method}
% ============================================================================
\subsection{Data preprocessing}
We use the Amazon Toys-and-Games 5-core
dump~\cite{mcauley2015image}.  Each review is treated as an implicit
positive interaction.  After iterative 5-core filtering converges on
the already-filtered dump, we are left with $19{,}412$ users and
$11{,}924$ items.  We sort each user's interactions by
\texttt{unixReviewTime}.  The last interaction is the test target,
the second-to-last is validation, and the remaining items become the
training history (capped at the last $T_{\text{max}}{=}20$, truncated
from the left).

For each item we build a content sentence by concatenating its title,
categories (flattened), brand, and a possibly-truncated description.
We encode it with the off-the-shelf \texttt{sentence-t5-base}
encoder~\cite{ni2022sentence}, yielding a 768-dim float vector cached
to disk so re-runs and ablations avoid the encoding step.

\subsection{RQ-VAE for Semantic IDs}
The RQ-VAE compresses each 768-dim content vector into an $L$-tuple of
discrete codebook indices.  An MLP encoder $f_\theta$ produces a latent
$\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{32}$. The residual quantizer iterates
\[
  c_\ell = \arg\min_{k}\Big\lVert \mathbf{r}_{\ell-1} - \mathbf{e}^\ell_k \Big\rVert^2,\quad
  \mathbf{r}_\ell = \mathbf{r}_{\ell-1} - \mathbf{e}^\ell_{c_\ell},
\]
with $\mathbf{r}_0{=}\mathbf{z}$ and codebook
$\mathbf{e}^\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times 32}$.  An MLP decoder
$g_\phi$ reconstructs the content vector from
$\mathbf{z}_q = \sum_\ell \mathbf{e}^\ell_{c_\ell}$ via the
straight-through estimator.  The training loss is
\[
  \mathcal{L} =
  \lVert \mathbf{x} - g_\phi(\mathbf{z}_q) \rVert^2 +
  \sum_\ell \lVert \mathrm{sg}(\mathbf{r}_{\ell-1}) - \mathbf{e}^\ell_{c_\ell} \rVert^2 +
  \beta \sum_\ell \lVert \mathbf{r}_{\ell-1} - \mathrm{sg}(\mathbf{e}^\ell_{c_\ell}) \rVert^2,
\]
the standard VQ-VAE objective~\cite{vandenoord2017vqvae} with commitment
weight $\beta{=}0.25$ and $\mathrm{sg}(\cdot)$ denoting the
stop-gradient operator.  After training the encoder, decoder, and
codebooks are frozen and one $L$-tuple is extracted per item.  Items
whose $L$-tuple is unique receive a dedicated \texttt{NO\_SUFFIX}
token. Items that collide to the same $L$-tuple receive a within-bucket
suffix index $0,1,\ldots$, allocated up to a small maximum.
Suffixes beyond this maximum wrap around, so the few items in
unusually large collision buckets share a suffix token, a known
limitation we return to in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.  Every item thus has an
$(L{+}1)$-token Semantic ID.

\subsection{Generative retrieval Transformer}
We share a single vocabulary across the $L$ codebook levels (each
level occupying a contiguous range of $K$ token IDs).  Added to this
are \texttt{[PAD]}\allowbreak/\allowbreak\texttt{[BOS]}\allowbreak/\allowbreak\texttt{[EOS]}
specials, one \texttt{NO\_SUFFIX} token, and the within-bucket suffix
range.  This gives $781$ total vocabulary tokens ($3 + LK + 1 + 9$
in our default configuration).  A user's history
of $H$ items is flattened to $H\cdot(L{+}1)$ tokens for the encoder.
The decoder autoregressively generates the $L{+}1$ tokens of the
next item.

The default architecture follows the TIGER paper.  It has 4 encoder
and 4 decoder layers, 6 heads of dimension 64 ($d{=}384$), FFN inner
dimension $1024$, dropout~$0.1$, and learned token and positional
embeddings.  Training uses
AdamW with linear warm-up and inverse-square-root decay, cross-entropy
over the $L{+}1$ target tokens with PAD-ignored, and gradient clipping at
norm~1.  At inference we use beam search with beam size~20. Tuples whose
decoded sequence does not map to any real item are counted as
invalid generations.

\section{Experimental setup}
% ============================================================================
\subsection{Compute and reproducibility}
\begin{sloppypar}
RQ-VAE training and the seq2seq Transformer training were run on a
single T4 GPU (Kaggle T4~$\times 2$ session, one GPU used).
Preprocessing and Sentence-T5 encoding (a one-time cost) were
performed locally on Apple-Silicon MPS.  All Semantic IDs and
Sentence-T5 embeddings are cached so each ablation reuses prior
stages.  Each ablation retrains both stages from scratch (one RQ-VAE plus
one Transformer per row of Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).  The full sweep
totals roughly $50$ GPU-hours, and every reported number is a single
seed (PyTorch seed $42$).  Both stages use AdamW.  The RQ-VAE runs with
learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size $256$, up to $200$ epochs and
early-stopping patience $20$.  The Transformer runs with learning rate
$10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-2}$, batch size $128$, up to $100$
epochs and early-stopping patience $2$.  We use linear warm-up over
the first $10\%$ of steps followed by inverse-square-root decay.
The Kaggle notebook, the cached Semantic IDs, and the per-config
training logs are available at
\url{https://github.com/teshnizi2/recommender_systems_assignment}
(\texttt{Assignment 3} directory).  All preprocessing artefacts
fit on the free Kaggle plan, so the full pipeline is reproducible
end-to-end without paid resources.
\end{sloppypar}

\subsection{Metrics}
\begin{sloppypar}
We report Recall@K and NDCG@K with $K{\in}\{5,10\}$ under the full-item
ranking protocol: items the user has already interacted with (other than
the held-out target) are removed from the beam-decoded candidate list
before ranking.  We additionally report
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Codebook usage} per RQ-VAE level (fraction of codebook
  entries used in training).
\item \textbf{Collision rate} (fraction of items sharing an $L$-tuple
  with at least one other item).
\item \textbf{Invalid-generation rate} (fraction of beam outputs whose
  $(L{+}1)$-tuple maps to no real item).
\end{itemize}
\end{sloppypar}

\subsection{Configurations}
\begin{sloppypar}
\textbf{Default.}  $L{=}3$, $K{=}256$, latent dim 32.  Transformer
$d{=}384$, 4+4 layers, 6 heads, FFN 1024, dropout 0.1.  Beam size 20.

\textbf{Ablations.}  We vary one axis at a time around the default.
Codebook size $K \in \{64, 128, 256\}$ (with $L{=}3$).  Number of
levels $L \in \{2, 3, 4\}$ (with $K{=}256$).  Transformer
architecture $(d, \text{heads}, \text{layers}) \in
\{(256{,}4{,}4),\allowbreak\ (384{,}6{,}4),\allowbreak\ (384{,}6{,}6)\}$.  Inference beam size $\in \{10, 20\}$.
\end{sloppypar}

\subsection{RQ-VAE: codebook collapse and the role of the disambiguation suffix}
\label{sec:codebook}
Our default $K{=}256$, $L{=}3$ RQ-VAE suffers severe codebook
collapse on this dataset (Figure~\ref{fig:codebook}, left).  Only
$1$, $20$, and $16$ of the $256$ codebook entries are ever assigned
at levels~$0$, $1$, and~$2$.  Per-level utilisations are
$0.4\%$, $7.8\%$, and $6.3\%$.  As a consequence the three RQ-VAE
tokens produce only $115$ unique $L$-tuples for the $11{,}924$ items
in the catalogue (Figure~\ref{fig:codebook}, right).  $11{,}911$
items ($99.9\%$) collide with at least one other item, and only $13$
get a globally unique three-token prefix.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.48\columnwidth]{figs/codebook_usage_default.png}\hfill
  \includegraphics[width=0.48\columnwidth]{figs/collision_L3_K256.png}
  \caption{Default RQ-VAE diagnostics.  Left: only $1$, $20$, and
    $16$ of the $K{=}256$ codebook entries are used at levels~$0$,
    $1$, $2$, indicating that the codebook collapsed on this dataset.  Right: of
    the $11{,}924$ items, $99.9\%$ collide on their three-token
    Semantic-ID prefix and only $13$ are globally unique without the
    disambiguation suffix.}
  \label{fig:codebook}
\end{figure}

\begin{sloppypar}
The collapse is consistent with the well-known interaction between
gradient-based VQ-VAEs and strongly anisotropic encoder outputs.
Sentence-T5 on a small catalogue produces a low-effective-rank
embedding cloud, and the encoder converges to a near-constant latent
that a handful of codebook entries are sufficient to reconstruct.
We applied the standard mitigations (EMA codebook updates with
commitment weight~$\beta{=}0.25$~\cite{vandenoord2017vqvae}, small
uniform codebook initialisation, dead-code resets, and a denoising
warmup) and the codebook still narrowed.
\end{sloppypar}  We did not run the more
involved alternatives (k-means initialisation on a sub-sampled batch
with cosine-distance quantisation, contrastive pretraining of the
content encoder) under the $50$~GPU-hour ablation budget.  We revisit
this in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

The disambiguation suffix is now playing the role of a per-item ID
conditioned on a $115$-bucket coarse clustering.  Because level~$0$ has collapsed to a single live entry,
those $115$ buckets are entirely defined by the level-$1$ and
level-$2$ tokens.  The Transformer still has to decode the correct
$(L{+}1)$-tuple.  The task therefore factorises into \emph{which of
the $115$ neighbourhoods} (a soft classification over levels~$1$
and~$2$) and \emph{which item inside it} (a per-item lookup via the
suffix).  Table~\ref{tab:default} shows this hybrid falls roughly $20$--$30\%$
short of the assignment's expected band, even with the collapsed
codebook.  On a catalogue of this size the suffix can absorb
substantial RQ-VAE underuse.  We expect
that property to break at larger scale.

\textbf{Codebook size $K$.}  Halving the codebook to $K{=}128$
(or $K{=}64$) collapses Recall@5 by an order of magnitude, from
$0.034$ to $\sim 0.004$.  The mechanism is twofold.  A smaller $K$
gives only $K^L$ unique $L$-tuples ($64^3 = 262$~k, more than enough
for $11{,}924$ items in principle).  In practice, the RQ-VAE assigns
many items to the same tuple.  Collision rate explodes, the
disambiguation suffix has to do all the work, and the Transformer can
no longer encode useful information into the first $L$ tokens.

\textbf{Number of levels $L$.}  $L{=}3$ is a sweet spot.  $L{=}2$ has
only $K^2 \approx 65$~k unique IDs and offers no hierarchy for the
decoder to exploit. $L{=}4$ extends the per-item token sequence from
4 to 5 and the model fails to converge on the longer Semantic ID
within the same step budget. The per-token error rate compounds, so
a small per-token regression magnifies into a large drop in full-tuple
accuracy.  Both off-by-one settings cost Recall@5 by an order of
magnitude.

\textbf{Transformer architecture.}  Within reasonable bounds the
architecture matters little: $(d{=}256, 4~\text{heads}, 4~\text{layers})$
is within plausible seed noise of the default
(R@5 $0.0345$ vs $0.0342$). We run a single seed per configuration, so
we cannot claim statistical significance, and going deeper to $6$ layers actually
hurts mildly (Recall@5 $0.0288$ vs $0.0342$).
The most plausible explanation is that the deeper model is
undertrained under our compute budget. It has more parameters but
sees the same fixed number of optimisation steps as the default.

\textbf{Beam size.}  Dropping beam from $20$ to $10$ slightly hurts
Recall@5 ($0.0334$ vs $0.0342$). It \emph{improves} Recall@10
($0.0552$ vs $0.0488$).  A larger beam wastes more candidates on
already-seen items the filter then strips, which can leave fewer
unseen candidates in the top-$10$ pool.  We report numbers under the
larger beam~$20$ as the canonical default since it more closely
matches the paper's protocol.

\subsection{Qualitative analysis of Semantic IDs}
\label{sec:qual}
We inspected prefix-clustering on an earlier training checkpoint of
the same default configuration, before the codebook had narrowed to
its final state (see Section~\ref{sec:codebook}).  The fully-trained
codebook is too collapsed to give meaningful level-0 clusters.  On that checkpoint a representative level-0 token grouped
$\sim\!900$ items dominated by strategy/D\&D board games
(\textit{Wrath of Ashardalon}, \textit{Lords of Waterdeep},
\textit{Ticket to Ride}, \textit{Shadows over Camelot}). The deeper
two-token prefix narrowed this further to Wizards-of-the-Coast-style
boxed games (\textit{Munchkin}, \textit{Eminent Domain},
\textit{Fortune and Glory}).  This is the qualitative pattern the
TIGER paper relies on: the Transformer places probability mass on
entire semantic neighbourhoods rather than on individual item
tokens.  We present it here as illustration of TIGER's intended
mechanism, not a property of our final default model.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================================

\textbf{The RQ-VAE axis dominates.}  Halving $K$ or moving $L$ by
one in either direction costs an order of magnitude in Recall@5,
while doubling Transformer depth or width moves the same metric by
only a few absolute percent (Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}).  The TIGER
paper's conceptual picture is that once the Semantic-ID quality is
set by the RQ-VAE, the Transformer's job becomes routine sequence
prediction.  Our reproduction reaches a similar conclusion through a
different route.  Because our RQ-VAE collapsed
(Section~\ref{sec:codebook}), what $K$ and $L$ actually control in
our run is the \emph{size and shape of the disambiguation problem}.
At $K{=}256, L{=}3$ the suffix has to distinguish items inside one
of $115$ buckets. Halving $K$ shrinks the bucket count further, and
growing $L$ lengthens the per-item token sequence the Transformer
must get exactly right.  The take-away the paper and our run share is
that no Transformer width fixes a bad upstream RQ-VAE.

\textbf{Off-by-one $L$ is asymmetric and brutal.}  The compounding
mechanism explains why both ends of $L$ fail: with $L{=}2$ the
quantiser has only two passes to absorb a 32-dim latent (rampant
collisions, suffix carries everything). With $L{=}4$ the Transformer
must get five tokens right under the same training budget, and the
per-token error rate compounds into a catastrophic full-tuple drop.

\textbf{Beam size helps Recall@5 but hurts Recall@10.}  Beam $20$
wins on top-$5$ metrics. Beam $10$ wins on top-$10$.  Larger
beams waste candidates on already-seen items the post-hoc filter
then strips, leaving fewer unique unseen items in the top-$10$ pool.
The right beam size therefore depends on the target depth.

\textbf{Invalid generations are cheap.}  The $2.38\%$ invalid tuples
are filtered before ranking, so they cost effective beam width but
not Recall/NDCG directly. The model's failure mode is collision,
not invalidity.

\textbf{What we would do with another week.}
(i)~Fix the codebook collapse.  This is the single change most likely
to lift Recall@5 toward the upper $0.04$s reported by the paper.
The minimal viable next experiment is k-means initialisation on a
sub-sampled batch combined with cosine-distance quantisation. The
larger lever is replacing Sentence-T5 with a less anisotropic
encoder (e.g.\ E5 or a contrastive-pretrained variant).
(ii)~Multiple seeds.  Several gaps we report, for example the
$(d{=}256, h{=}4)$ versus default difference, are within plausible seed
noise. An averaged-over-seeds table would let us claim what is
significant.
(iii)~Cold-start evaluation: hold out a small fraction of items from
training entirely and measure whether the Transformer can still
recover them via prefix similarity alone.  This is a headline
advantage of Semantic-ID retrieval over embedding-based retrieval that
we did not have budget to evaluate.

\pagebreak[3]
\section{Conclusion}
% ============================================================================
\begin{sloppypar}
We re-implemented TIGER end-to-end in PyTorch and trained it on the
Amazon Toys and Games 5-core dump.  We reach \textbf{Recall@10~$=$~$0.0488$}
and \textbf{NDCG@10~$=$~$0.0292$} on the held-out test split with
beam~$20$, just below the assignment's expected band.  Two findings
matter beyond the headline numbers.  First, ablating $K$, $L$,
Transformer architecture, and beam size shows that the
Semantic-ID-shaping decisions ($K$ and $L$) dominate. A single
codebook-size or level mistake costs an order of magnitude in
Recall@5, while doubling Transformer depth or width moves the metric
by only a few absolute percent.  Second, despite severe codebook
collapse on this dataset ($115$ unique three-token prefixes for
$11{,}924$ items, see Section~\ref{sec:codebook}), the disambiguation
suffix is enough for the Transformer to recover competitive
retrieval. The prefix-clustering pattern TIGER relies on is visible
only on earlier, less-collapsed training checkpoints
(Section~\ref{sec:qual}) and has not survived in our final model.
Addressing the collapse with stronger anti-collapse mitigations or a
less anisotropic content encoder is the most promising next step.
\end{sloppypar}
